# 01 — What is a story?

*Storytelling lens 1. Status tags (Decided / Proposed / Open) are defined in [../index.md](../index.md).*

## Working thesis

**[Decided]** A story is a causally connected sequence of changes to a situation, driven by desire meeting resistance, where selection and arrangement create meaning. In interactive fiction the author's job shifts from authoring events to authoring a *possibility space*: the arc emerges from how the world responds to the player's choices. The world and the things around the player are crafted; the player has agency to move through them as they please.

## The governing philosophy

**[Decided]** The player controls what they do, but not what the world does. The player is a cog in the machine, and the world moves without them. If the player goes off the rails, the big bad and every other questline keep resolving in the background, possibly to the detriment of the player or the world, because no one intervened.

**[Proposed]** Shorthand: *the world has momentum; the player has leverage.* A cog does not command the machine, yet a story needs the protagonist's choices to matter. So the world must respond to leverage: a player who engages can bend, delay, redirect or break trajectories; a player who does not watches them play out.

**[Decided]** Whether the protagonist is "the chosen one" (prophecy, plot armor, a destiny that bends toward them) is *not* an engine question. The default is no, and the answer belongs to setting and campaign design. The engine stays neutral and only has to be able to express such a stance.

**[Decided]** Campaigns are long and the world evolves. Sessions work toward a main goal that may be modest ("complete the dungeon"), eventually "defeat the big bad", after which a new big bad can arise. One-shots must also be supported.

## Classical foundations and what they imply

- **Aristotle (Poetics).** Plot is the arrangement of incidents; events follow by probability or necessity; reversal and recognition are the most powerful moments. *Implication:* offscreen events must be derived from causes (a faction's goals and resources), not rolled at random. A world that moves without the player produces reversal (the village you left has burned) and recognition (learning who was behind it) naturally.
- **Forster.** "The king died, and then the queen died" is a sequence; "...died of grief" is a plot. *Implication:* the event log must record *why*, not just *what*. The strongest form is the **consequence of omission**: "the caravan was lost because no one escorted it."
- **Fabula vs. discourse (Russian Formalists, Genette).** What happens in the world versus how and when it is told. *Implication:* the simulation holds the truth, and the DM chooses how the player learns it (a rumor, a refugee, a smoking ruin, a letter that arrives too late). **Information propagation** is a core storytelling concern: an offscreen event that nothing carries to the player might as well not have happened.
- **Todorov.** Equilibrium, disruption, new equilibrium. *Implication:* a one-shot is one cycle; a campaign is cycles nested inside cycles ("complete the dungeon" inside "defeat the big bad", then "a new big bad arises").

## Factions are characters

**[Decided]** Factions and kingdoms have as much desire and agency as individuals. The story lives where their desires collide with the player's.

**[Open]** How the actions of an individual within a faction shape that faction's qualities. See [03](03-character-desire-opposition.md).

## Telegraphing the world

**[Proposed]** Fairness requires that the world signal what is coming, so that going off-rails is an informed choice with real costs and not a gotcha. This must be done through signals about the world, never through lists of options or consequences (see [04](04-agency-and-tenets.md)).

**[Open]** Grim portents and countdown clocks (as in Dungeon World / Blades in the Dark) have been looked at. Ficus does not want to formalize any such mechanism yet and wants to explore options first.
